 The kakapo is critically endangered, with only 247 living individuals known, all of which have been given names. The ancestral kakapo population was once widespread on the main islands of New Zealand, but is now confined to modified habitats on islands free of predation by invasive mammals. The kakapo was largely forgotten until the 1970s, but by the 1980s conservation efforts began in earnest. New populations have been established on three islands, and the kakapo population has increased steadily. The conservation plan is to establish at least two further kakapo populations on two new islands, and to maintain the current high survival rate of adults.

The kakapo has a well-noted nocturnal lifestyle, and was not widely recognised in New Zealand until the 1840s. It was heavily hunted by Māori, and by European settlers who prized it as a specimen for museums. The introduction of predators such as cats, rats, and stoats during European colonisation almost wiped out the kakapo. By the 1970s, only a single declining population remained on Stewart Island.
