 The kakapo is critically endangered, with only 249 living individuals known, all of which have been given names. The ancestral kakapo migrated to the islands of New Zealand when they split off from the supercontinent Gondwana, around 82 million years ago. Because it was the only land mammal around at the time, it had no predators, and lost the need to fly. It developed strong legs to climb trees, and wings that only serve for balance. When Polynesian and European settlers came to New Zealand, they brought with them many invasive species such as cats, rats, and stoats, which are the main reasons for the kakapo's decline. Because the kakapo reproduces so slowly, it cannot replenish its population quickly enough to keep up with the rate at which it is being eaten. Conservation efforts began in 1894, but they were not very successful until the Kakapo Recovery Programme began in 1995. The conservation efforts mainly focus on keeping the kakapo on several predator-free islands, and relocating the entire population to a new island when an invasive species is discovered on their current island.
