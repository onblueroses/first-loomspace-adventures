 

The kakapo is critically endangered, with only 249 living individuals known, all of which have been given names. The common ancestor of the kakapo and the genus Nestor became isolated from the remaining parrot species when New Zealand broke off from Gondwana, around 82 million years ago. Around 70 million years ago, the kakapo diverged from the genus Nestor. In the absence of mammalian predators, the kakapo lived in relative abundance in the forests of New Zealand until the arrival of humans. It was heavily hunted by the Māori, the indigenous people of New Zealand, who also deforested much of the islands to provide habitat for human settlements. Europeans also contributed to the decline of the kakapo when they settled in New Zealand in the 19th century, as they hunted the kakapo for museum specimens and for their skins and feathers, which were used to make items of clothing. Habitat destruction also continued during European settlement. In 1894, the New Zealand government installed Richard Henry as the caretaker of Resolution Island, an uninhabited island in the south-west of the South Island of New Zealand, charging him with the task of preserving the kakapo and other endangered native birds.
