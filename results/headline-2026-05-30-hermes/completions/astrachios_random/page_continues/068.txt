 The kakapo is critically endangered, with only 249 living individuals known, all of which have been given names. The ancestral kakapo population was once widespread across the islands of New Zealand, but it has been eliminated from almost all of its original range since human settlement of the country began several hundred years ago. Because of Polynesian and European colonisation and the introduction of predators such as cats, rats, ferrets, and stoats, most of the kakapo population was wiped out. Conservation efforts began in the 1890s, but they were not very successful until the implementation of the Kakapo Recovery Programme in 1995. As of November 2022, the total known population is 249 living individuals, most of which have been given names.

The kakapo has a well-documented history of breeding: a frontline account was written in the 1930s by pioneer conservationist Richard Henry, who noted that they attempted to breed every second year when certain native trees (such as the rimu) were heavy with fruit. The kakapo is a nocturnal and flightless bird, making it one of the least-known birds in the world. It is also one of the longest-living birds in the world, with an average lifespan of 60 years
