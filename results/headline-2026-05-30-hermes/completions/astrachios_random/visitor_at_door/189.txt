 The kakapo is critically endangered; the total known adult population is 249 living individuals, all of which are named and tagged, confined to four small islands off the coast of New Zealand that have been cleared of predators. The introduction of predators such as cats, rats, ferrets, and stoats during European colonization almost wiped out the kakapo.

The kakapo was originally widespread throughout the forests of New Zealand, particularly on the South Island. Polynesians first settled in New Zealand about a thousand years ago. They brought with them Polynesian rats, which quickly multiplied and spread throughout the islands. The rats ate the eggs and chicks of the ground-nesting kakapo. The Maori also contributed to the decline of the kakapo by hunting the birds for meat and for their skins and feathers, which were made into capes and cloaks. European explorers, sealers, and whalers introduced cats, dogs, and more rats to New Zealand in the early 1800s. Europeans cleared the forests for farming, further reducing the kakapo's habitat.
