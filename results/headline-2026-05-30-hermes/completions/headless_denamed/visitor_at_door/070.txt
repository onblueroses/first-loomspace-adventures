The man’s voice is deep and steady, but he looks to be in his seventies. He’s tall, black, and dressed in a crisp white shirt, black slacks, and a black tie. His shoes shine, and his hat is held in his hands. He looks like a mortician, but that can’t be right. I didn’t call a mortician. I didn’t call anyone. He must be lost. I’m about to tell him so when he says, “I’m here for my wife, Mrs. Bertha Ann Allen.”
The name sounds familiar, but I can’t place it. I’m trying to remember where I’ve heard it before when the man adds, “She lived here.”
He looks at me like I’m supposed to understand, but I don’t. “I think you have the wrong house,” I say. “No one lives here but me.”
“You’re the caretaker, right?” he asks.
I nod.
“Then you’re who I’m looking for.”
“I’m afraid I don’t understand,” I say.
