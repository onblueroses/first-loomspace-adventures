 What has passed through the door into the room is not only a man who seems to be about to die but a figure of the _already dead_, who will nonetheless die again, as if for the first time. (Caruth 1996: 31)
In other words, the figure that enters the room is not just the wounded man, but a figure of the already dead; a ghost. As Caruth argues, the repetition of the man's death in the dream, the fact that he dies again, "as if for the first time," suggests that the origin of the trauma is in this repetition, in the way the man's death is experienced over and over again, in the very fact that he is already dead, and yet will die again. It is this incomprehensibility, the dream's refusal to be placed within a clear temporal or spatial framework, that constitutes the dream's traumatic core.
Caruth's reading of the dream thus emphasizes the way in which the dream resists interpretation, the way in which the origin of the trauma is in the very incomprehensibility of the event, the way in which the dream cannot be situated in a clear time or place.
