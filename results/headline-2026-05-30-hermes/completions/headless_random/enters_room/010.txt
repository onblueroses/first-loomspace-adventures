 The Earth's crust is made up of many large plates. These plates are in constant motion, slowly moving around the Earth's surface. The movement of these plates is called plate tectonics.

The theory of plate tectonics was first proposed in the early 20th century by a German scientist named Alfred Wegener. He noticed that the continents seemed to fit together like a puzzle, and he suggested that they were once joined together in a single landmass called Pangaea.

Over time, the theory of plate tectonics has been refined and supported by a growing body of evidence. Today, it is widely accepted by scientists as the explanation for many of the Earth's geological features.

The movement of the plates is driven by convection currents in the Earth's mantle. These currents are caused by the heat generated by the decay of radioactive elements deep within the Earth.

As the plates move, they interact with each other in various ways. When two plates collide, it can cause the formation of mountains or volcanic activity. When two plates move apart, it can create new oceanic crust.
