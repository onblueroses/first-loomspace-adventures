 

The Earth's crust is a thin shell on the outside of the Earth, accounting for less than 1% of Earth's volume. It is the top component of the lithosphere, a division of Earth's layers that includes the crust and the upper part of the mantle. The lithosphere is broken into tectonic plates that move, allowing heat to escape from the interior of the Earth into space.

The crust is of two different types. Oceanic crust is thin and composed primarily of silicate igneous rocks: basalt, diabase, gabbro. Continental crust is thicker and composed primarily of less dense silicate igneous rocks: granite, granodiorite, and andesite. Both types of crust are less dense than the mantle below. Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust owing to their different compositions. Because of its density, oceanic crust generally lies below sea level, while continental crust projects above sea level.

The temperature of the crust increases with depth, reaching values typically in the range from about 100 °C (212 °F) to 600 °C (1,112 °F) at the boundary with the underlying mantle. The crust and underlying relatively rigid upper mantle make up the lithosphere.
