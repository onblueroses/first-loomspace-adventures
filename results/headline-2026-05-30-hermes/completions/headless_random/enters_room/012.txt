## 4
At the start of the 20th century, various theories were proposed concerning the formation of mountains. One of these was the shrinking Earth theory, which postulated that the wrinkles on the Earth's crust were formed by the gradual shrinking and cooling of the Earth. This theory was supported by the observation that the wrinkled surface of a dried-up apple could be smoothed out if the apple was swelled up by injecting water into it. The shrinking Earth theory was eventually replaced by the theory of continental drift, which was proposed by Alfred Wegener in 1912. Wegener's theory proposed that the continents were once joined together in a single landmass called Pangaea, which later broke apart and drifted to their present positions. However, Wegener's theory was not widely accepted at the time because he could not explain the mechanism behind the movement of the continents.

In the 1950s and 1960s, a new theory called plate tectonics emerged, which built upon Wegener's idea of continental drift. Plate tectonics proposed that the Earth's lithosphere, which includes the crust and the uppermost part of the mantle, is divided into several large plates that move relative to each other.
