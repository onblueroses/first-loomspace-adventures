 The Earth's lithosphere, which is the rigid outermost shell of a planet, is broken into tectonic plates. The lithosphere is divided into several plates, some of which are very large and others which are smaller. The largest plates are the Pacific Plate, the North American Plate, the Eurasian Plate, the Antarctic Plate, and the African Plate. These plates move relative to one another at speeds ranging from a few millimeters to a few centimeters per year. The movement of these plates is driven by forces generated by the Earth's internal heat.

The plates are composed of the oceanic lithosphere and the thicker continental lithosphere, each topped by its own kind of crust. Along convergent boundaries, the process of subduction carries one plate down under another and recycles oceanic crust back into the mantle. By this process, the total surface area of the lithosphere remains roughly the same. This prediction of plate tectonics is also referred to as the conveyor belt principle. Earlier theories, since disproven, proposed gradual shrinking or gradual expansion of the globe.
