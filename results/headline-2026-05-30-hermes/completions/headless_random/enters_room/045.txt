 Earthquakes, volcanic activity, mountain-building, and oceanic trench formation occur along these plate boundaries or faults. In this situation, extensional forces caused by convection currents in the underlying asthenosphere, cause a plate to break into two or more smaller plates. The now-divergent plate boundaries are still seismically active, but most of the seismicity is within a few hundred kilometers of the boundary. As the new divergent boundary develops, one or more triple junctions form. The triple junction is unstable so it splits into three divergent boundaries. The three boundaries then continue to diverge until they impact another boundary, where a subduction zone or a transform boundary forms.

The boundary between the Pacific Plate and the North American Plate is an example of one such boundary. The two plates do not collide, but instead one plate is subducted under the other, which is known as a subduction zone. The subducted plate is heated and partially melted as it is forced deeper into the mantle, and the resulting molten material is forced to the surface in volcanic eruptions.
