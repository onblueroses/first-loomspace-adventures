 Tectonic plates, the Earth's lithosphere is broken into tectonic plates. They are made up of the oceanic lithosphere and the thicker continental lithosphere, each topped by its own kind of crust. The plates move relative to each other, typically at rates of zero to 100 millimeters annually. The relative movement determines the type of plate boundary: convergent, divergent, or transform. Earthquakes, volcanic activity, mountain-building, and oceanic trench formation occur along these plate boundaries or faults. Subduction carries one plate down under another and recycles oceanic crust back into the mantle. The lithosphere has greater mechanical strength than the underlying asthenosphere, allowing the plates to move due to convection currents in the mantle. At a seafloor spreading ridge, plates move away from the ridge, cooling and increasing in density as they move away. The total surface area of the lithosphere remains roughly the same due to this process, known as the conveyor belt principle. The theory of plate tectonics builds on the concept of continental drift and was validated by the proposal of seafloor spreading in the late 1950s and early 1960s.
