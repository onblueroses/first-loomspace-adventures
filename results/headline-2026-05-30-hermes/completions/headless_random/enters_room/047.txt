In the early 20th century, German meteorologist Alfred Wegener proposed the theory of continental drift, which posited that the continents were slowly drifting around the Earth. Wegener's theory was initially met with skepticism, but it sparked a new interest in the study of the Earth's crust.

In the 1950s and 1960s, a series of discoveries provided strong evidence for the theory of plate tectonics. In 1953, American geologist Maurice Ewing and his team discovered the presence of a deep trench in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean, which they named the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. This discovery led to the realization that the ocean floor was not flat, but instead was marked by a series of underwater mountain ranges and valleys.

In 1962, American geologist Harry Hess proposed the theory of seafloor spreading, which suggested that new oceanic crust was being formed at the mid-ocean ridges and then slowly spreading outwards. Hess's theory was supported by the discovery of magnetic stripes on the ocean floor, which showed that the Earth's magnetic field had reversed itself many times in the past.
