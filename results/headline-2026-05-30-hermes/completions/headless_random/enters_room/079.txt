 

The Earth's crust, or lithosphere, is broken into tectonic plates that move slowly over time. These plates are composed of oceanic and continental crust, with the latter being thicker. At the boundaries where plates meet, different geological processes occur. Convergent boundaries involve one plate being forced under another in a process called subduction, which recycles oceanic crust back into the mantle. Divergent boundaries occur where plates move away from each other, such as at mid-ocean ridges where new crust is formed. Transform boundaries involve plates sliding past each other horizontally, often causing earthquakes. The motion of tectonic plates is driven by convection currents in the Earth's mantle, caused by heat from the Earth's core. This slow movement of the solid mantle is what allows the plates to move over time. The theory of plate tectonics explains many geological phenomena, such as the formation of mountains, earthquakes, and the distribution of fossils and rock types around the world. It builds upon earlier ideas of continental drift and seafloor spreading, which were validated by scientific evidence in the mid-20th century. The theory has since been widely accepted by the scientific community.
