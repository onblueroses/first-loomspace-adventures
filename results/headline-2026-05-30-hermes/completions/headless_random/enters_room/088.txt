 

This is the Earth's lithosphere. It's a rigid outer shell that is broken up into tectonic plates. These plates move around, and where they meet, we have different types of boundaries, such as convergent, divergent, or transform. The relative movement of the plates is typically slow, ranging from zero to 100 millimetres annually. Earthquakes, volcanic activity, mountain-building, and oceanic trench formation occur along these plate boundaries or faults.

Tectonic plates are composed of oceanic lithosphere and thicker continental lithosphere, each topped by its own kind of crust. Along convergent boundaries, subduction carries plates down under another and recycles oceanic crust back into the mantle. This keeps the total surface area of the lithosphere roughly the same, a concept referred to as the conveyor belt principle.

Tectonic plates can move because the Earth's lithosphere has greater mechanical strength than the underlying asthenosphere. Lateral density variations in the mantle result in convection currents, the slow creeping motion of Earth's solid mantle.
