 

The theories of plate tectonics and continental drift were not initially accepted by the scientific community, but by the 1960s the evidence for both theories became irrefutable. In 1912, Alfred Wegener proposed the theory of continental drift, which he based on several lines of evidence. One was the match of large-scale geological features on different continents, such as mountain ranges that seemed to line up when the continents' coastlines were aligned. Wegener also noted the similarity of rock ages and similarity of fossilized plants and animals in corresponding geological layers in now-separated continents. The existing paradigm at the time was that mountains formed as the Earth cooled and shrank. It was difficult for Wegener to explain how continents could plow through the much denser rock of oceanic crust.

The first evidence that the lithosphere was divided into plates that move relative to one another came from seismology. In the 1950s and 1960s, scientists used data from earthquakes and developed seismology and paleomagnetism to map the ocean floor. This led to the discovery of seafloor spreading by Harry Hammond Hess and confirmation by independent data that the Earth's magnetic field had reversed itself in the past.
