 The cosmological constant is the value of the energy density of the vacuum of space. It was originally introduced by Albert Einstein in 1917 as an addition to his theory of general relativity to achieve a static universe. Einstein abandoned the concept after the observation of the Hubble redshift indicated that the universe might not be static, but expanding. Now the cosmological constant is invoked to explain the observed acceleration of the expansion of the universe.

The cosmological constant is the simplest possible form of dark energy, since it is constant in both space and time. The cosmological constant is denoted by the Greek capital letter lambda: Λ. The measured values of the cosmological parameters show that the introduction of dark energy, and in particular of a cosmological constant, is necessary to describe the universe.

The cosmological constant can be formulated in terms of Planck units as Λ = 2.89 × 10−122 tP−2 (with tP the Planck time). The cosmological constant appears in Einstein's field equation in the form of R − 2Λ = 8πGT, where R is the Ricci tensor, G is Newton's gravitational constant, and T is the stress–energy tensor.
