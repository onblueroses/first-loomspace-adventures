 

Tectonic plates follow a "conveyor belt" model, with plates being created at ridges and subducted at trenches. This is known as the Wilson cycle. The global average rate of new crust formed at ridges is equal to that being recycled into the mantle at subduction zones. This is why the Earth has not grown significantly larger or smaller over time. 

The main driving force of plate tectonics is gravity. At ridges, hotter and thus less dense material rises to the surface, pushing plates apart. At trenches, old, cold, dense plates sink back into the mantle, pulling the rest of the plate along with them. This creates a "conveyor belt" effect, with new crust being created at ridges and old crust being recycled at trenches. 

The Earth's lithosphere is divided into several large plates, which move relative to each other at rates ranging from a few millimeters to a few centimeters per year. The boundaries between these plates are marked by earthquakes, volcanoes, and other geological features.
