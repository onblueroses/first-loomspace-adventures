 There are various ways to explore and understand the phenomenon of plate tectonics, which is the scientific theory that describes the large-scale motion of Earth's lithosphere. This lithosphere, which is the rigid outermost shell of our planet, is broken up into what are called tectonic plates. These plates move relative to each other, and where they meet, we find the formation of various geological features and phenomena such as earthquakes, volcanic activity, mountain-building, and oceanic trenches.

The concept of plate tectonics has its roots in earlier ideas such as continental drift, which was proposed in the early 20th century. However, it was not until the late 1950s and early 1960s that the theory of plate tectonics came to be generally accepted by geoscientists, following the validation of the concept of seafloor spreading.

The Earth's lithosphere is composed of seven or eight major plates, along with many minor plates. The relative movement of these plates typically ranges from zero to 100 millimetres annually. The type of boundary that forms between two plates is determined by their relative motion, which can be convergent, divergent, or transform.
