 The earth's mantle is a rocky shell about 2,900 km (1,800 mi) thick that constitutes about 84% of Earth's volume. It is predominantly solid and encloses the iron-rich hot core, which occupies about 15% of Earth's volume. The mantle is divided into several layers based on changes in mineral phase and rheology, which are influenced by increasing pressure and temperature with depth. The uppermost layer is the Moho, a seismic discontinuity that marks the sharp increase in velocity of seismic waves as they pass from the crust into the denser mantle. Beneath the Moho, the upper mantle extends from the base of the crust down to a depth of about 410 km (250 mi). The transition zone extends from a depth of about 410 km (250 mi) down to roughly 660 km (400 mi). The lower mantle extends from a depth of about 660 km (400 mi) down to roughly 2,700 km (1,700 mi). The base of the lower mantle, at a depth of about 2,700 km (1,700 mi), is defined by a sudden increase in seismic wave velocities, known as the D" discontinuity.
