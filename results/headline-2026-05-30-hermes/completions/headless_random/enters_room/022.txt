So, let's say that the Earth's lithosphere is made up of a number of tectonic plates, which are large slabs of solid rock. These plates move relative to each other, causing earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and the creation of mountains and ocean trenches. The movement of these plates is driven by convection currents in the Earth's mantle, which is the layer beneath the lithosphere. The plates are composed of oceanic and continental crust, and when they collide, one plate can be forced underneath another in a process called subduction. This recycles the oceanic crust back into the mantle, keeping the Earth's surface area relatively constant. The reason the plates can move is because the lithosphere is stronger than the asthenosphere, the layer below it, allowing the plates to slide over it. The movement of the plates away from spreading ridges, where new crust is formed, also contributes to their motion. This theory, called plate tectonics, is now widely accepted and explains many of the Earth's geological features. (5)

The theory of plate tectonics revolutionized our understanding of the Earth's geology. Before this theory, scientists struggled to explain how and why continents and oceans were distributed across the globe
