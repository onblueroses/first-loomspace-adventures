The Earth's surface is divided into several rigid plates, each of which moves relative to one another over the underlying, hotter, more fluid asthenosphere. The plates move at a rate of a few centimeters per year, about the same rate as your fingernails grow. There are seven major plates, as well as a number of minor ones. These plates are composed of the Earth's crust and a portion of the upper mantle known as the lithosphere. The lithosphere is cooler and more rigid than the asthenosphere, which allows the plates to move.

The movement of these plates is driven by forces originating in the Earth's interior. These forces include the heat and convection of the mantle, which causes the plates to move in different directions. This movement can cause the plates to collide, pull apart, or slide past each other. When two plates collide, it can result in the formation of mountains, like the Himalayas. When they pull apart, new crust is formed, as seen in the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. When they slide past each other, it can result in earthquakes, such as those experienced in California.
