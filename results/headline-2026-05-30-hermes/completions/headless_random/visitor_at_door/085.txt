The Earth's outer shell, the lithosphere, is not a single, solid piece but is broken up into several large tectonic plates that move slowly over time. This movement is driven by the heat and convection patterns in the Earth's mantle beneath the plates.

At divergent boundaries, two plates move away from each other, and new oceanic crust is formed by volcanic activity, filling in the gaps. This process is known as seafloor spreading. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is a prime example of this type of boundary.

Convergent boundaries occur when two plates collide with each other. The collision can result in one plate subducting or diving under the other, leading to the formation of deep oceanic trenches, volcanic arcs, and mountain ranges. The subducted plate is recycled back into the Earth's mantle.

Transform boundaries are where two plates slide past each other horizontally, creating friction and stress that can lead to earthquakes. The San Andreas Fault in California is a well-known example of a transform boundary.
