 "Please come in," he says. "I have something to show you." He leads you into a room filled with maps, globes, and diagrams. "This is the Earth," he says, pointing to a large globe in the center of the room. "And these are the tectonic plates." He gestures to a series of irregular shapes covering the globe's surface.

"The Earth's crust is broken into these plates, which are constantly moving and interacting with each other. This movement is driven by convection currents in the mantle below the crust." He points to a diagram showing the layers of the Earth, with arrows indicating the movement of molten rock in the mantle.

"At the boundaries where plates meet, we see different types of geological activity. Where plates are moving apart, we get rift valleys and new oceanic crust forming at mid-ocean ridges. Where plates are colliding, we get mountain ranges like the Himalayas or subduction zones where one plate is forced under another, causing volcanic activity."

He traces his finger along a convergent boundary on the globe. "The Pacific Ring of Fire is a prime example of this, with numerous volcanoes and earthquakes along the plate boundaries."
