The Earth's lithosphere is divided into seven or eight major plates and many minor plates. These large plates move slowly, at a rate of about 10 centimeters per year. This movement is caused by convection currents in the mantle, which is the layer of the Earth below the crust. The heat from the Earth's core causes the mantle to rise, cool, and then sink back down, creating a circular motion. This motion causes the plates to move.

At the boundaries where these plates meet, different geological events can occur. If the plates are moving towards each other, it is called a convergent boundary. This can cause one plate to be pushed under the other, a process called subduction. This often leads to the formation of volcanoes and mountain ranges.

If the plates are moving away from each other, it is called a divergent boundary. This often happens at mid-ocean ridges, where new oceanic crust is formed as the plates pull apart. This process is known as seafloor spreading.

If the plates are sliding past each other, it is called a transform boundary. This can cause earthquakes, as the plates can get stuck and then suddenly move, releasing energy.
