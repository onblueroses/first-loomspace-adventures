The discovery of radioactivity and the development of its applications to geological dating (see below) in the early 20th century provided a new basis for arguing that the Earth was billions of years old, rather than the 20 million years suggested by Kelvin's calculations. The new data provided by radioactive measurements initially gave ages of 400 million to 2 billion years, and caused a revolution in scientific thought about the age of the Earth.

The discovery of radioactivity introduced another factor in the calculation. After Henri Becquerel's initial discovery in 1896, Marie and Pierre Curie discovered the radioactive elements polonium and radium in 1898; and in 1903, Pierre Curie and Albert Laborde announced that radium produces enough heat to melt its own weight in ice in less than an hour. Geologists quickly realized that this upset the assumptions underlying most calculations of the age of Earth.
