 

Plate tectonics describes the bulk motions of the lithosphere, the outermost layer of Earth, which is broken up into a number of tectonic plates. The bulk motions of these plates is driven by convection currents in the mantle, the layer just below the lithosphere. Convection currents in the mantle are driven by the heat generated by the decay of radioactive isotopes, which causes the mantle to convect from the bottom up, like water in a pot heated on a stove.

The relative motion of the plates causes a variety of phenomena, including earthquakes, volcanism, mountain building, and the formation of oceanic trenches. Plate tectonics also has economic implications, as the locations of mineral deposits and fossil fuels are often controlled by the tectonic history of an area.

The theory of plate tectonics was first proposed in the early 20th century, but it was not until the 1960s that it gained widespread acceptance. The development of plate tectonics theory was one of the most important scientific advances of the 20th century, as it provided a unifying framework for understanding the geology of Earth.
