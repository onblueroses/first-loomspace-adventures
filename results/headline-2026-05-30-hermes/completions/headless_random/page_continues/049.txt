#### 2.2.4.3   Continental drift

Continental drift is the movement of the Earth's continents relative to each other, thus appearing to drift across the ocean bed. The speculation that continents might have drifted was first put forward by Abraham Ortelius in 1596. The concept was independently and more fully developed by Alfred Wegener in 1912. The hypothesis of continental drift became part of the larger theory of plate tectonics in the mid-20th century.

The Earth's lithosphere is divided into several tectonic plates that are in constant slow motion. The seven major plates are the African, Antarctic, Eurasian, North American, South American, India-Australia, and the Pacific plates. Plate boundaries are of three types: convergent boundaries, divergent boundaries, and transform boundaries. Earthquakes, volcanic activity, mountain-building, and oceanic trench formation occur along these plate boundaries. The relative movement of the plates typically ranges from zero to 100 millimetres annually.

Continental drift is a very slow process, with plates moving about 2 to 5 centimetres horizontally each year. However, when measured over geological time, the movement of the continents is quite significant.
