 

The relative motion of the plates causes crustal deformation along plate boundaries. This motion is driven by a combination of sources: the gradient in gravity from the ridge to the trench, the net slab pull down at subduction zones, and the net trench suction that drives plates toward one another. Plate motions are also thought to be driven by a combination of forces generated by the Earth's rotation and mantle convection. These forces induce both plate movement and plate deformation.

In 1973, George W. Moore of the USGS and R. C. Bostrom presented evidence for the "Gregg-Moore Plate" (alternatively, the "Bostrom Plate" or the "Shatsky Plate") beneath the northeastern Pacific Ocean. This region is believed to be a relic plate that was subducted beneath the Pacific Plate and is currently located in the lower mantle. 

The outer shell of the Earth is made up of plates that are in constant motion. This motion is driven by a combination of forces generated by the Earth's rotation and mantle convection. The relative motion of the plates causes crustal deformation along plate boundaries, which can result in earthquakes, volcanic activity, mountain-building, and oceanic trench formation.
