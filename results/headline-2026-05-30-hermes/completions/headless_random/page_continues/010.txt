The Earth's crust is divided into several large tectonic plates. These plates are in constant motion, and this motion is driven by forces from within the Earth's mantle.

Where plates meet, we can have convergent boundaries, where the plates move towards each other, divergent boundaries where the plates move apart, and transform boundaries where the plates move sideways with respect to each other.

At convergent boundaries, one plate can be forced below the other in a process known as subduction. This can result in volcanos forming on the overriding plate as the sinking plate melts and the magma rises through the overriding plate. The Andes mountains are an example of this type of plate boundary.

Divergent boundaries occur at mid-ocean ridges, where plates are moving apart. This allows magma to rise and create new crust, resulting in underwater volcanos.

Transform boundaries are regions where plates are moving sideways with respect to each other. The San Andreas fault in California is an example of this type of boundary.
