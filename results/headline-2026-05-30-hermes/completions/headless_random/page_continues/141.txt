The Earth's plates are in constant motion, driven largely by heat from the Earth's interior.

The mantle, which lies between the Earth's crust and its core, is made of solid rock, but over long periods of time, it behaves like a slow-moving liquid. This is known as convection, and is similar to the process by which hot water near the bottom of a pan rises, while cooler water near the top sinks.

Convection in the mantle is the driving force of plate tectonics. Hot material rises from the mantle and erupts along mid-ocean ridges to create new seafloor. This process is known as seafloor spreading. It slowly pushes the plates apart.

Over time, the seafloor spreads and moves away from the mid-ocean ridges, eventually cooling and becoming denser. The denser, cooler plates then sink into the mantle at places called subduction zones. Subduction zones are found where plates collide, such as along the western coast of South America.
