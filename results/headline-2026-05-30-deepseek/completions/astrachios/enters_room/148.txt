 The woman who stands in the doorway is tall and gaunt. Her face is a skull's, and her eyes are deep-set, sunken, her mouth a lipless gash.

She wears a long grey robe.

She says, "My name is Liza Hempstock. My friends call me Old Mrs. Hempstock. You can call me Mrs. Hempstock."

I say, "I'm sorry. I thought you were a dream."

She smiles, in the way a skull would smile, if a skull could smile. "Then dream me," she says. "Dream the fire and the blood and the farmhouse. Dream the pain."

I say, "I did. I dreamed it all."

She says, "Sometimes, when the night is a friend, you dream true. Sometimes you don't." She sniffs the air. "You woke the ocean," she said. "I wondered who had."

I say, "I needed to get home."

She says, "You were lost. You're not lost anymore. You're here. I told you to follow the ocean if you got lost, and you did. You're here."

She walks into the room and sits down on the bed. The bedsprings creak
