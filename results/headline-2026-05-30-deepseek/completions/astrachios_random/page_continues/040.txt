Its moss-green plumage, mottled with yellow and black, provides excellent camouflage in the forest undergrowth. The kakapo has a distinctive facial disc of fine feathers, similar to an owl, which aids in directing sound to its ears—a trait that helps this nocturnal bird navigate in the dark. It possesses a strong, musty odor, described as sweet and earthy, which unfortunately makes it easy for introduced mammalian predators to locate.

The kakapo's decline began with human settlement. Māori and, later, European settlers brought rats, cats, and stoats, against which the slow-moving, ground-nesting kakapo had no defense. Habitat destruction further reduced its numbers. By the 1990s, the species was nearly extinct, with only 51 known individuals.

A remarkable and intensive recovery program, managed by New Zealand's Department of Conservation, has brought the kakapo back from the brink. All remaining birds have been relocated to predator-free offshore islands like Codfish Island (Whenua Hou) and Anchor Island. Each bird is closely monitored and given individual names.
