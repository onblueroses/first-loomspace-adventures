Its moss-green plumage is mottled with yellow and black on the upperparts, providing excellent camouflage against the forest floor. The kakapo has a distinct facial disc of fine feathers, similar to an owl, which contributes to its excellent night vision. Its wings are relatively small for its body size and are used for balance and support rather than flight, often employed when climbing trees.

The kakapo has a strong, sweet, musty odor, which has been described as like the smell of honey and old violin cases. This scent likely serves as a means of communication between individuals but makes them easily detectable by introduced mammalian predators, a key factor in their decline.

Kakapos are exceptionally long-lived, with some individuals known to live over 90 years. They have a slow reproductive rate, breeding only every two to four years when certain forest trees, such as the rimu (*Dacrydium cupressinum*), produce a mast (bumper crop) of fruit.
