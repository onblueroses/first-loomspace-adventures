The kakapo's unique characteristics and adaptations to its island environment have made it a fascinating subject for conservationists. Due to its inability to fly, it evolved as a ground forager, feeding on native plants, seeds, and fruits. Its nocturnal habits and moss-green coloration provide camouflage in the forest undergrowth.  

Unfortunately, the arrival of humans and introduced predators (such as rats, cats, and stoats) led to a severe population decline. By the 1990s, the species was nearly extinct, with only around 50 individuals remaining.  

An intensive recovery program was established, involving relocation to predator-free offshore islands (like Whenua Hou/Codfish Island), careful monitoring, and supplementary feeding. Thanks to these efforts, the population has slowly increased—exceeding 250 birds as of recent counts—though the species remains critically endangered.  

The kakapo's breeding cycle is closely tied to the fruiting of certain trees, such as the rimu, which occurs only every few years. During breeding seasons, males gather in arenas called leks, where they produce deep, resonant "booming" calls to attract females.
