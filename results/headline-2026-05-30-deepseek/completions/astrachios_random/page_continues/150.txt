The kakapo's unique combination of traits has made its conservation particularly challenging. As a flightless bird, it evolved without mammalian predators, which left it highly vulnerable when humans introduced rats, cats, and stoats to New Zealand. Its nocturnal and ground-dwelling habits increase its exposure to these threats.

The lek breeding system means males gather in specific areas to display and compete for females through deep, resonant "booming" calls that can travel kilometers. However, this also makes them easier for predators to locate. Their slow reproductive rate—females nest on the ground and raise chicks only every 2–4 years when certain forest trees like the rimu fruit abundantly—further hinders population recovery.

Conservation efforts, led by New Zealand's Department of Conservation, include relocating all remaining kakapos to predator-free offshore islands (such as Codfish Island and Anchor Island), intensive monitoring, supplementary feeding, and artificial incubation of eggs. Thanks to these measures, the population has slowly increased from a low of about 50 in the 1990s to around 250 today, though the species remains critically endangered.
