The kakapo's unique traits—flightlessness, nocturnal habits, and specialized breeding system—reflect its adaptation to New Zealand's isolated, predator-free environment before human arrival. Its herbivorous diet consists of native plants, seeds, and fruits, and it uses a distinctive "lek" mating system where males gather and compete for females through booming calls.  

Due to introduced predators like rats, cats, and stoats, along with habitat loss, the kakapo nearly went extinct. Intensive conservation efforts, including relocation to predator-free islands, controlled breeding, and close monitoring, have helped the population slowly recover, though it remains critically endangered with only around 250 individuals as of recent counts.  

Its evolutionary lineage is also notable: genetic studies suggest the kakapo split from other parrots around 82 million years ago, making it one of the most ancient parrot species alive today.
