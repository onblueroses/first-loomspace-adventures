The kakapo’s unique traits are adaptations to its island environment, where it evolved without mammalian predators. Its moss-green plumage provides camouflage in the forest undergrowth, and its strong legs allow it to climb trees and walk long distances. However, these adaptations became disadvantages with the arrival of humans and introduced predators like cats, rats, and stoats, leading to a severe population decline.

Conservation efforts, led by New Zealand’s Department of Conservation, involve relocating kakapos to predator-free offshore islands, intensive monitoring, and assisted breeding programs. Each bird is individually named and tracked. Despite these efforts, with only around 250 individuals as of recent counts, the kakapo remains one of the world’s rarest birds. Its recovery is slow due to low genetic diversity and infrequent breeding, which is tied to the cyclical fruiting of certain native trees.
