Its plumage is a distinctive moss-green mottled with yellow and black, providing superb camouflage in its forest and scrubland habitats. This camouflage, combined with its nocturnal habits, makes it notoriously difficult to observe.

The kakapo has a highly developed sense of smell, which it uses to locate food, and a distinctive, musty-sweet odor. Males gather in traditional arenas during the breeding season and produce a deep, resonant "booming" call that can travel several kilometers to attract females. This breeding cycle is infrequent and tied to the mast fruiting of specific forest trees, particularly the rimu (*Dacrydium cupressinum*).

Historically widespread, the kakapo's population was devastated following human colonization of New Zealand due to introduced predators like stoats, cats, and rats, as well as habitat destruction. By the 1990s, the species was functionally extinct on the mainland.

All surviving kakapos have been translocated to predator-free offshore islands: Whenua Hou/Codfish Island, Puke Nui/Anchor Island, and Te Hauturu-o-Toi
