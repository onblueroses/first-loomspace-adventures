The kakapo's unique biology is further highlighted by its adaptations to a predator-free island environment before human arrival. Its moss-green plumage provides excellent camouflage against the forest floor, and it has a highly developed sense of smell. Unlike most birds, it has a low metabolic rate and can store large amounts of body fat, contributing to its heavy weight. It is also one of the longest-living bird species, with a potential lifespan exceeding 90 years.

Reproduction is infrequent and linked to the mast fruiting of specific forest trees, particularly the rimu. During breeding seasons, males congregate in leks, where they excavate shallow bowls and produce a deep, resonant "booming" sound to attract females. After mating, the female raises the chicks entirely alone.

The primary causes of its critically endangered status are introduced predators (such as cats, rats, and stoats), habitat destruction, and its inherently low reproductive rate. Intensive conservation efforts are underway, including a successful transfer of all known individuals to predator-free offshore islands (like Whenua Hou/Codfish Island and Anchor Island), managed breeding programs, and supplementary feeding. As of the latest official census, the total population is slowly increasing but remains below 250 mature individuals.
