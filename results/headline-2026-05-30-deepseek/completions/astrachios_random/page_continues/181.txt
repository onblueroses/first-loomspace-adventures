The kakapo's unique characteristics are a result of its long isolation in New Zealand, an environment free of mammalian predators until human arrival. Its adaptations—flightlessness, cryptic green plumage for camouflage in forest undergrowth, and a strong, musty odor—served it well in that ancient ecosystem. However, these same traits made it catastrophically vulnerable to introduced predators like rats, cats, and stoats brought by Polynesian and European settlers.

Its breeding system is particularly specialized. Males gather in arenas (leks) and perform loud, low-frequency "booming" calls to attract females. After mating, the female alone builds the nest, incubates the eggs, and raises the chicks for up to six months. This infrequent breeding is often tied to the mast fruiting of specific forest trees, meaning successful nesting may only occur every few years.

Conservation efforts, led by New Zealand's Department of Conservation, are intensive and represent one of the most complex wildlife recovery programs in the world. The entire known population resides on predator-free offshore islands (such as Whenua Hou/Codfish Island and Anchor Island).
