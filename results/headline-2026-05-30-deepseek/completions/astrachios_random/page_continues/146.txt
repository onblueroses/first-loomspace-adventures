The kakapo's unique adaptations reflect its evolution in an island environment with no native land mammals. Its inability to fly likely developed in the absence of mammalian predators, and its nocturnal habits are unusual for a parrot. Its strong, musky odor may have evolved as a scent-based communication system in dense forest undergrowth.

Sadly, these traits made it extremely vulnerable when humans introduced predators like cats, rats, and stoats to New Zealand. Combined with habitat loss, this led to a catastrophic population decline. By the 1990s, the species was nearly extinct.

Today, the entire remaining population (around 250 birds as of 2024) lives on predator-free offshore islands like Codfish Island (Whenua Hou) and Anchor Island. The Kakapo Recovery Programme, a decades-long intensive conservation effort involving the New Zealand Department of Conservation and Māori iwi (tribes), manages every individual bird. This includes monitoring nests with technology, supplementary feeding to boost breeding, and a pioneering use of artificial insemination.
