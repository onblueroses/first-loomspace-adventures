The kakapo's unique combination of traits has made it both fascinating and vulnerable. Its inability to fly, a trait that evolved in the absence of land mammals, left it defenseless when humans introduced predators like rats, cats, and stoats to New Zealand. This is a primary reason for its critical endangerment.

The bird's unusual **lek breeding system** involves males gathering in an arena to compete for female attention through loud, low-frequency "booming" calls that can travel several kilometers. This breeding is also tied to the irregular fruiting of certain trees, like the rimu, meaning successful breeding seasons are infrequent.

Conservation efforts, led by New Zealand's Department of Conservation, have been intensive and innovative. The entire known population has been moved to predator-free offshore islands (such as Whenua Hou/Codfish Island and Anchor Island). Each bird is closely monitored, and management includes supplementary feeding to boost breeding. Thanks to these efforts, the population has slowly increased from a low of about 50 individuals in the 1990s to over 250 today.
