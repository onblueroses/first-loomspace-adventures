Its appearance is distinctive, with moss-green feathers barred with black and yellow on the upper parts, providing excellent camouflage in its native forest and scrubland habitats. The kakapo has a large, grey beak, short legs, large feet, and relatively short wings that are useless for flight. It has a facial disc of fine feathers, similar to an owl, which aids its nocturnal habits, and a well-developed sense of smell.

The kakapo's history is marked by a drastic decline following human colonization of New Zealand. It evolved in the absence of terrestrial mammalian predators, which left it vulnerable to introduced species such as cats, rats, and stoats. Habitat destruction further pressured the population. By the 1990s, the species was brought to the brink of extinction, with only 51 known individuals.

A remarkable, intensive conservation program managed by the New Zealand Department of Conservation (DOC) has since pulled the species back from that edge. All remaining kakapos have been transferred to predator-free offshore islands: Whenua Hou/Codfish Island, Anchor Island, and Hauturu-O-Toi/Little Barrier Island.
