The kakapo’s unique traits have evolved in the absence of mammalian predators, which were absent in New Zealand until human arrival. Its nocturnal and ground-dwelling habits, along with its moss-green mottled plumage, provide excellent camouflage in the forest undergrowth.  

When threatened, the kakapo freezes in place, relying on its cryptic coloration to avoid detection—a strategy effective against native avian predators like eagles, but less so against introduced mammals such as cats, stoats, and rats.  

Its **lek breeding system** is particularly notable: males gather in arenas where they compete for female attention by emitting deep, resonant “booms” that can travel several kilometers. This breeding behavior, combined with a slow reproductive rate (females nest only every 2–4 years when certain forest trees like the rimu fruit abundantly), has contributed to its decline.  

Conservation efforts led by New Zealand’s Department of Conservation (DOC) include **intensive management** on predator-free offshore islands, supplementary feeding to encourage breeding, and the use of **smart transmitters** to monitor individuals. The kakapo recovery program has seen some success, with the population slowly increasing from a low of about 50 birds in the 1990s to over 200 today
