The kakapo’s unique traits are closely tied to its evolutionary history in New Zealand, an island ecosystem that lacked terrestrial mammals (aside from bats) before human arrival. This allowed birds like the kakapo to fill ecological niches typically occupied by mammals elsewhere, evolving flightlessness, ground-nesting, and a slow, energy-conserving lifestyle.  

Its **polygynous lek breeding system** involves males gathering in arenas to compete for female attention through loud, low-frequency “booming” calls that can travel several kilometers. Females choose mates, then raise chicks entirely alone.  

As a **functional herbivore**, the kakapo has a strong, grinding gizzard and a diet consisting mainly of native plants, seeds, fruits, and occasionally insects. Its metabolism is exceptionally slow for a bird, contributing to a long lifespan—some individuals exceed 90 years.  

**Conservation status:**  
Historically, the kakapo ranged across New Zealand, but introduced predators (rats, cats, stoats) and habitat loss caused catastrophic decline. By the 1990s, only about 50 individuals remained.
