The kakapo’s unique traits and behaviors have made it a fascinating subject for conservation biologists. Its **lek breeding system** involves males gathering in an arena to compete for female attention through vocal displays—a rare behavior among parrots.  

Being **functionally herbivorous**, it feeds on native plants, seeds, fruits, and even tree bark. Unlike many birds, it accumulates significant fat reserves, contributing to its heavy weight.  

Historically, the kakapo’s inability to fly and ground-nesting habits made it vulnerable to introduced predators like rats, cats, and stoats. By the 1990s, the population dropped to around 50 individuals. Intensive conservation efforts—including relocation to predator-free islands (such as Whenua Hou/Codfish Island), supplementary feeding, and monitored breeding programs—have slowly increased numbers to over 200 today.  

The kakapo is also known for its longevity, with some individuals living over 90 years. Each bird is carefully tracked and named by conservation staff.
