The kakapo's unique combination of traits has made it an iconic conservation symbol. Its evolutionary path diverged when New Zealand's lack of mammalian predators allowed it to become large, flightless, and ground-nesting. This became a severe disadvantage with the introduction of rats, cats, and stoats by humans, leading to a catastrophic population decline.

Conservation efforts, led by New Zealand's Department of Conservation, are intensive and innovative. All remaining kakapos have been relocated to predator-free offshore islands like Whenua Hou/Codfish Island and Anchor Island. Each bird is individually named and monitored. The recovery program uses supplementary feeding and meticulous management of the breeding cycle, which is tied to the fruiting of specific trees like the rimu, which occurs only every 2-4 years.

As of the latest 2024 data, the total population is approximately 250 living individuals, a significant but fragile increase from a low of around 50 in the 1990s. The kakapo's future remains uncertain, hinging entirely on continued, active human intervention.
