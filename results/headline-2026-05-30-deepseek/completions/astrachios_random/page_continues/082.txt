Its moss-green plumage, mottled with yellow and black, provides excellent camouflage against the forest floor. The kakapo has a distinctive facial disc of fine feathers, similar to an owl, which aids in directing sound to its ears. This adaptation is crucial for its nocturnal lifestyle, as it possesses an exceptional sense of smell but relatively poor eyesight.

Kakapos are long-lived birds, with some individuals known to live over 90 years. They have a slow reproductive cycle, breeding only when certain forest trees, like the rimu, produce a heavy fruit crop—an event that may occur only every two to four years. During the breeding season, males emit a deep, resonant booming call that can travel several kilometers to attract females to their mating arenas, or leks.

The species' decline is primarily attributed to introduced mammalian predators such as rats, cats, and stoats, against which its evolved defenses of camouflage and freezing are ineffective. Habitat loss also played a significant role. By the 1990s, the kakapo was extinct on the mainland, surviving only on a few predator-free offshore islands.
