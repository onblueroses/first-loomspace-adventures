The kakapo’s unique traits have made its conservation both a challenge and a global priority.  

Its inability to fly, coupled with a strong, sweet scent (which helps males attract mates but also makes them easy for introduced predators to track), left it extremely vulnerable when humans, along with rats, cats, and stoats, arrived in New Zealand.  

By the 1990s, the population had dwindled to around 50 individuals. Through intensive conservation efforts—including relocation to predator-free islands, supplementary feeding, and careful monitoring—the number has slowly increased to around 250 birds as of recent counts.  

The kakapo’s breeding biology is also remarkable. They breed only when certain forest trees like the rimu produce a mast (heavy crop) of fruit, which may happen every two to four years. Males gather in arenas called leks, where they dig bowls in the ground and produce deep, booming calls that can travel kilometers to attract females. After mating, the female alone incubates the eggs and raises the chicks.  

Conservation programs now use artificial insemination, egg-fostering, and tailored nutrition to boost chick survival. Each kakapo is individually named and tracked, making it one of the world’s most closely managed species
