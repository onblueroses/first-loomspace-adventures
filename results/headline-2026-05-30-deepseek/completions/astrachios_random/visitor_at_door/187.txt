 "We're here to see the kakapo."

The woman in the office says, "I'm sorry, but we are not open to visitors."

"We have permission from the Department of Conservation," the visitor says. "They told us to come here."

The woman frowns. "I'll need to see your papers."

The visitor hands her a folder. She examines its contents.

"I see," she says. "Well, then. Come in."

She leads them through a door into a large room filled with cages. In each cage is a kakapo. The birds are all asleep, their heads tucked under their wings.

"This is the holding area," the woman says. "We have twenty-four kakapos here. They are all that are left in the world."

The visitor looks at the birds. They are all the same: green, with a face like an owl. They look like they are made of moss.

"We are working to save them," the woman says. "But it is difficult. They are very fragile."

She leads them through another door into a smaller room. In the center of the room is a large glass enclosure. Inside the enclosure is a single kakapo. It is awake, and it is looking at them.
