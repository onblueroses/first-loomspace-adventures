 "I'm sorry to disturb you. I'm looking for the scientist in charge of the kakapo recovery programme."

"Who is it?" I say. I am sitting at the table, writing this book.

"It's the Prime Minister," says my wife, looking out the window.

"Tell him I'm busy," I say.

She goes to the door, opens it, and speaks to him. He is not alone. There are journalists with him, photographers.

"Come in," she says.

They troop in.

He comes to me. "I want to congratulate you," he says. "You've done a wonderful job."

"I'm sorry," I say. "I'm not the one you want to talk to. I'm not the scientist in charge of the kakapo recovery programme."

"Oh," he says. "Then who is?"

"I don't know," I say.

He looks around the room. "Who is in charge of the kakapo recovery programme?" he asks.

We all shrug.

"Nobody is in charge?" he says.

"Nobody," I say.

"That's a bit strange, isn't it?"

"It is," I say. "But that's how it is."
