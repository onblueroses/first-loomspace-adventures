 Is it a person? Is it an animal? Is it a monster? Write about it in second person.

The door swings open slowly, and you freeze mid-sip, the lukewarm tea in your mouth suddenly forgotten. The air in the room changes first—it doesn’t grow cold, but heavy, as if the space between molecules has thickened with anticipation. A scent arrives before the form: damp earth, ozone, and something else, something sweetly metallic like old coins or rain on hot pavement.

It steps into the light from the hallway, and your brain struggles to parse the shape. It is not a person. The proportions are all wrong—the limbs too long and jointed in too many places, the torso a compressed, barrel-chested thing. It is not an animal you know. The skin, if it is skin, is the color of a storm cloud at dusk, a deep, mottled grey-purple that seems to drink the light rather than reflect it. It has no fur, no scales, but a texture like smoothed river stone.

And it is not a monster, not in the way you imagined them. There is no malice in its posture, no bared teeth or claws. It moves with a terrifying, liquid grace
