 The Earth's lithosphere, broken into tectonic plates, slowly moving over the planet's surface, driven by mantle convection currents. As these plates interact, they create boundaries where earthquakes, volcanoes, and mountain ranges form, shaping the Earth's surface over billions of years.

The plates move across the Earth's surface at a rate of up to 100 millimeters per year. They are driven by convection currents in the mantle, which slowly circulate due to heat from the Earth's core. This movement leads to the formation of new crust at mid-ocean ridges and the destruction of old crust at subduction zones, where one plate dives beneath another.

As the plates move, they interact at their boundaries. Divergent boundaries, where plates move apart, create new crust and can form rift valleys and mid-ocean ridges. Convergent boundaries, where plates collide, result in subduction zones, mountain ranges, and volcanic arcs. Transform boundaries, where plates slide past each other, cause earthquakes along faults like the San Andreas Fault in California.

The theory of plate tectonics explains many geological phenomena, including the distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes, the formation of mountain ranges, and the fossil record that shows similar species on continents now separated by oceans.
