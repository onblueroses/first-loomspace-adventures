## Plate tectonics: When we discovered how the Earth really works

The plate tectonics revolution shrank the world and enlarged our understanding of it, says Professor Iain Stewart.

In the 1960s, scientists discovered plate tectonics – and finally understood how continents move.

Until then, the maps in school atlases seemed carved in stone: the continents and oceans fixed and eternal. Yet there had been rumblings. As early as 1620, Francis Bacon had noted the jigsaw fit of the continents either side of the Atlantic. By 1915, German meteorologist Alfred Wegener was arguing that today’s continents were once assembled into a supercontinent he called Pangaea. His evidence was compelling: matching fossil assemblages, similar glacial scratches on now far-flung rocks, and complementary coastlines.

But Wegener’s continental drift lacked a mechanism. How could continents plough through solid oceanic crust? The breakthrough came from mapping the ocean floor. During World War Two, the US Navy financed oceanographic surveys that revealed the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, a submarine mountain chain. Subsequent surveys showed that oceanic crust was young and active, with new seafloor being created at mid-ocean ridges and old crust being destroyed at deep ocean trenches
