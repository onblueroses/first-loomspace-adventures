 "The Earth's lithosphere, which includes the crust and upper mantle, is made up of a series of pieces, known as tectonic plates. These plates move on a hot flowing mantle layer called the asthenosphere, which is several hundred kilometres thick. Heat within the asthenosphere creates convection currents that cause tectonic plates to move several centimetres per year relative to each other. When two plates come together, one of two things may happen. One plate may move under the other, a process known as subduction. Deep ocean trenches are often associated with subduction zones. The friction between plates prevents them from just sliding past each other. Instead, they build up pressure until they finally release, causing earthquakes. Where plates slide past each other, transform faults develop. Where plates move apart, they produce gaps that are filled by magma from below, creating new crust. Where plates collide, they may produce mountain ranges."

The Wikipedia page for "Plate tectonics" describes the Earth's lithosphere as being broken into tectonic plates, with seven or eight major plates and many minor plates. It explains that plate movement ranges from zero to 100 mm annually and that earthquakes, volcanic activity, mountain-building, and oceanic trench formation occur along plate boundaries.
