 Plate movement is thought to be driven by a combination of the motion of the seafloor away from spreading ridges due to variations in topography and density changes in the crust (density increases as newly-formed crust cools and moves away from the ridge). At subduction zones, the relatively cold, dense oceanic crust sinks down into the mantle, forming a subduction zone. These two forces act together to drive plate movement, overriding the influence of mantle convection.

The relative importance of each of these factors and their relationship to each other is unclear, and still the subject of much debate. The forces driving plate motion are:

- Ridge push (buoyant upwelling mantle at mid-ocean ridges)
- Slab pull (gravitational force of subducting plates)
- Slab suction (local flow in the mantle induced by downgoing plates)
- Mantle drag (shearing at the base of plates)
- Trench suction (gravitational force drawing plates into subduction zones)

The plate boundaries are identified based on the relative motion between plates: divergent (moving apart), convergent (moving together), and transform (moving sideways). Earthquakes, volcanic activity, mountain-building, and oceanic trench formation occur along these plate boundaries or faults.
