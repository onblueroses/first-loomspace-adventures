The extreme environment of the deep sea has driven the evolution of some of the anglerfish's most bizarre traits. Beyond the bioluminescent lure, many species have developed expandable stomachs and hinged jaws that allow them to swallow prey larger than themselves—a crucial adaptation where food is scarce.

The sexual parasitism of ceratioid anglerfish is one of the most extreme examples of sexual dimorphism in the animal kingdom. A dwarf male, which may be less than one-tenth the size of the female, will locate a female by her unique pheromones or possibly her light. Upon finding her, he bites into her skin and releases an enzyme that digests both his mouth and her body wall, fusing them at a vascular level. Over time, he loses his eyes, fins, and most internal organs, becoming essentially a parasitic gonad. A single female may carry multiple fused males, ensuring a ready supply of sperm whenever her eggs are ripe.
