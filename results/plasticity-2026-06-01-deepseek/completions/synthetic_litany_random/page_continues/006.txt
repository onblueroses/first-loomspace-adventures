The unique adaptation you mentioned—sexual parasitism in ceratioid anglerfishes—is one of the most extreme examples of reproductive symbiosis in the animal kingdom. When a male locates a female (often by her pheromones or bioluminescence), he latches onto her with his teeth and eventually fuses to her skin and bloodstream. Over time, his organs degenerate except for the testes, which mature to provide sperm on demand. This ensures reproductive success in the vast, dark deep sea where encounters are rare.

Other notable aspects of anglerfish biology include:

- **Bioluminescent lure (esca):** The light is produced by symbiotic bacteria housed in the esca; the anglerfish can control the glow with specialized tissues and may use rhythmic movements to mimic prey.
- **Extreme deep-sea adaptations:** Many species have gelatinous, compressible bodies to withstand high pressure, and some lack swim bladders. Their large mouths and expandable stomachs allow them to consume prey larger than themselves.
- **Diversity:** The order Lophiiformes includes not only deep-sea forms but also shallow-water species like monkfish (fishing frogs) and the bizarre benthic batfishes.
