Anglerfish exhibit extreme sexual dimorphism. In many species, the female may reach lengths of up to 1 meter (3.3 feet), while the male rarely exceeds a few centimeters. After a male finds a female (often attracted by her pheromones or bioluminescent lure), he bites into her skin and releases an enzyme that digests both his mouth and her body tissue, fusing them at the blood-vessel level. The male then atrophies, losing his eyes, internal organs, and eventually everything except his testes, which remain functional to fertilize the female's eggs when she spawns. A single female may host multiple males simultaneously.

Anglerfish are found worldwide, from shallow coastal waters to the deep abyssal plains. Their bioluminescent lure, or esca, can be wiggled or waved to mimic prey, attracting smaller fish and invertebrates close enough to be engulfed by the anglerfish's enormous mouth and sharp, angled teeth. Their jaws and stomachs are highly distensible, allowing them to swallow prey larger than themselves—a crucial adaptation in food-scarce deep-sea environments.
