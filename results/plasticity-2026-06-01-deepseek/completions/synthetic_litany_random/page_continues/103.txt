The anglerfish is a remarkable example of deep-sea adaptation and symbiosis. Its most famous feature, the bioluminescent lure (esca), is a sophisticated hunting tool that attracts prey in the perpetual darkness. This light is produced not by the fish itself, but by colonies of symbiotic bacteria housed within the lure—a partnership where the bacteria gain nutrients and a protected environment.

The extreme sexual dimorphism and parasitic mating strategy of some deep-sea species (like ceratioids) is a direct adaptation to the vast, sparse environment of the abyss. The tiny male permanently fuses to the female, effectively becoming a dedicated source of sperm and ensuring reproductive success when encounters are rare.

These traits—bioluminescent lures, symbiotic relationships, and parasitic reproduction—highlight the incredible evolutionary pressures and unique solutions found in the deep ocean's ecosystems.
