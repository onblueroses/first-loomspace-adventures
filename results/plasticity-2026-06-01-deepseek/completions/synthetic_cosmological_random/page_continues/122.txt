The consequences of such human alterations are profound and often cascade through the entire riverine food web. Damming a river, for instance, fragments the longitudinal continuum, transforming a flowing lotic environment into a series of stagnant, lake-like reservoirs upstream while drastically altering the flow, temperature, and sediment regime downstream. This directly eliminates habitat for species adapted to swift currents and disrupts critical life-cycle migrations of fish like salmon. The reduction of natural sediment transport can lead to downstream channel erosion and the loss of delta-building processes at the river's mouth.

Similarly, channelization—straightening and hardening banks with concrete or riprap—severs the lateral connection between the river and its floodplain. This simplification of habitat eliminates backwaters and side channels that serve as crucial nurseries for juvenile fish and reduces the system's capacity to process nutrients and organic matter. Agricultural runoff introduces excess fertilizers (nitrates and phosphates) and pesticides, which can lead to eutrophication in slower reaches. The resulting algal blooms can cause severe diurnal swings in dissolved oxygen, suffocating fish and other aquatic life.
