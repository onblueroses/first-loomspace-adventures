Human modifications often disrupt the natural flow regime—the seasonal pattern of high and low water that river organisms are adapted to. Dams, for example, fundamentally alter this regime by stabilizing flows, trapping sediment and nutrients upstream, and releasing water of a different temperature. Downstream, these changes can eliminate critical spawning cues for fish, reduce habitat complexity by preventing gravel replenishment, and favor generalist species over those specialized for natural river conditions.

Similarly, channelization—the straightening and hardening of banks for flood control or navigation—severs the river from its floodplain. This process eliminates backwaters and wetlands that serve as nurseries for juvenile fish and refuges during droughts or floods. It also accelerates water velocity, increasing erosion downstream and simplifying in-stream habitat by removing pools, riffles, and woody debris.

Pollution inputs constitute another major pressure. Agricultural runoff carries excess nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus) and fine sediments. The nutrients can fuel excessive algal growth (eutrophication), leading to nighttime oxygen depletion as algae decompose.
