**Human Impacts and Management**

The alteration of river ecosystems by human activity is often profound. Dams, constructed for hydropower, irrigation, or flood control, are among the most significant modifications. They fragment the river continuum, blocking the migration of fish and the downstream transport of sediment and nutrients. The resulting reservoirs create lentic conditions, altering temperature and oxygen regimes upstream, while reduced sediment flow can lead to channel erosion and coastal land loss downstream.

Channelization—the straightening and hardening of banks with concrete or rock—aims to control flooding and drain land. However, it severely degrades habitat complexity, eliminates riparian zones, and accelerates water flow, which increases erosion and reduces water-quality filtration.

Non-point source pollution from agricultural and urban runoff introduces excess nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus), pesticides, and heavy metals. This can lead to eutrophication, algal blooms, and oxygen-depleted "dead zones," particularly in slower-moving lower reaches. Point-source pollution from industrial or municipal discharges adds toxic compounds and thermal pollution.

**Conservation and Restoration**

Effective river management requires an integrated, catchment-scale approach.
