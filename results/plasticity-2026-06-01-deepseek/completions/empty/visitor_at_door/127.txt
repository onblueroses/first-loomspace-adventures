 "I am a student at the University of California, and I am studying the history of Japanese-Americans in the United States. I would like to ask you some questions." The housewife, a Japanese-American, answers, "I'm sorry. I don't want to be interviewed." The student persists, "But I only want to ask you a few questions about yourself and your parents." "I don't want to be bothered," says the housewife, and she closes the door. (Kitano, 1976: 6)

The student in this scenario is probably perplexed, and perhaps even annoyed, at the housewife's reluctance to talk. Yet, the housewife's behavior is typical of many Japanese-Americans, particularly among the older generations. This behavior reflects the Japanese cultural emphasis on privacy and reticence, and the fact that Japanese-Americans have suffered greatly from discrimination and hostility in the United States. The housewife's response also illustrates the fact that Japanese-Americans have become a largely invisible group in American society. They are not highly concentrated residentially, they hold a variety of jobs, and they have achieved a high degree of acceptance among other Americans.
