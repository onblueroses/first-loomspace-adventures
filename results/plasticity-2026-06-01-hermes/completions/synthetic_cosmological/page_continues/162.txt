 "This is the invocation proper, which, if correctly pronounced, will cause all the spirits to be subject to the speaker, and to do whatever he shall command them. But it must be pronounced in the accents of the ancient language, and care must be taken to utter each word and name correctly, without pause or stumbling, for if there be error, the speaker will be subject to the spirits, and destroyed by them. After the invocation, let the speaker pronounce his commands to the spirits in the language of his own day, and they shall be obeyed."

The invocation, while doubtless impressive, is hardly more so than many others in the book, and is certainly less flamboyant than some. The passage that follows it, however, is strikingly different from anything else in the volume. It is in the same language and orthography as the rest of the work, but it is written in a smaller, tighter hand, and with far less precision, as if it had been set down in haste. It is also in a different voice, that of the author addressing the reader directly, and in a tone of urgency. It reads as follows:
