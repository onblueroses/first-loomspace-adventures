The door opens. It is I who stand there, but not as I am now, in this room, in this world, but rather as I was, as I am, in the room where I write these words, in the world I inhabit as I write them. I am older, grayer, more stooped. I wear glasses and my hands are gnarled with age. I step into the room, the room of my youth, the room of my memory, and I sit down at the table, across from my younger self, who stares at me in wonder and in fear.

"You're me," he says. "But older."

"Yes," I say. "I am you, but older. I have come to tell you something important."

"What is it?" he asks, his voice trembling.

"I have come to tell you that you must not fear the forgetting," I say. "You must not fear the loss of memory, the loss of self. For in the forgetting, there is also a remembering, a remembering of what is truly important, of what is truly eternal."

"I don't understand," he says. "How can forgetting be remembering?"
