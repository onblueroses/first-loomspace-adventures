 "The luminescence comes from symbiotic bacteria, which are thought to be acquired from seawater, that dwell in and around the esca."

The esca is the "lure" at the tip of the modified dorsal fin ray. The bacteria are not the same species as the fish. They are different organisms that live in a symbiotic relationship with the fish. The fish provides a suitable environment for the bacteria to live in, and the bacteria provide light for the fish's lure. This is an example of mutualism, a type of symbiosis where both organisms benefit from the relationship.

Anglerfish are not the only marine animals that use bioluminescent bacteria for various purposes. Other examples include certain species of squid, cuttlefish, and some deep-sea shrimp. The bacteria often live in specialized light organs in these animals, and the light produced can be used for attracting prey, communication, or camouflage.

In many cases, the animals are able to control the light emission, turning it on or off as needed. The specific mechanisms for this control vary between species but can involve physical barriers that block the light, changes in the supply of oxygen or nutrients to the bacteria, or even chemical signals that modulate the bioluminescence.
