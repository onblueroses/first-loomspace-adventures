 "In a study by Dr. Theodore W. Pietsch, University of Washington, of 120 known species of ceratioids, only 25 have been documented well enough to know whether the males do or do not become permanently attached. Of these 25 species, 16, or 64%, feature permanent attachment. The temporary attachments, for the most part, involve the male only latching onto the female without tissue fusion."

This is a fascinating example of a symbiotic relationship between two organisms, and I find it particularly interesting how the male anglerfish becomes a permanent appendage to the female. The fact that this is the case for 64% of the well-documented species is also noteworthy. It makes me wonder about the evolutionary advantages and disadvantages of such an extreme form of sexual parasitism. The male anglerfish, by fusing with the female, ensures a constant supply of nutrients and a readily available mate for reproduction. However, this also means that the male is entirely dependent on the female for survival, and if the female dies, the male will also perish. Additionally, the limited mobility of the fused male may reduce the chances of encountering other females for mating, potentially reducing genetic diversity within the population.
