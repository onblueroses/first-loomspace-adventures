 The light gland is always open to the exterior, so it is possible that the fish acquires the bacteria from the seawater. However, it appears that each species of anglerfish has its own particular species of bacterium, and these bacteria have never been found in seawater. Haygood (1993) hypothesized that esca discharge bacteria during spawning and the bacteria are thereby transferred to the eggs. The bacteria then enter the developing eggs and proliferate within the light organ. This is supported by the fact that in some species, the bacteria are vertically transmitted from mother to egg, ensuring that the bacteria are transferred to the next generation. In other species, the bacteria are present in the surrounding seawater and are acquired by the developing eggs and juvenile fish. The source of the bacteria is not well understood, but it is thought that they are transferred from the environment to the fish through ingestion or contact with the skin or gills. The bacteria then colonize the light organ and begin to multiply, eventually producing the luminescence that is characteristic of anglerfish.

In summary, the bacteria that produce the luminescence in anglerfish are acquired from the surrounding seawater, either through vertical transmission from the mother to the eggs or through ingestion or contact with the skin
