 

Some anglerfish, like those of the genus Ceratias, are notable for extreme sexual dimorphism and sexual symbiosis, in which the much smaller male becomes permanently attached to the female and lives as a sperm-providing outgrowth. In these species, the male's attachment to the female is followed by atrophy of the blood vessels, internal organs, and even the brain, until only the reproductive organs and a lump of tissue that links the male to the female remain. The males of the anglerfish family Phrynichidae show a marked difference in the size of the olfactory organs, with the males having six to ten times the number of odor-sensitive lamellae as the females. The sensitive olfactory organs help the males to detect the pheromones that signal the proximity of a female anglerfish. Females remain large to accommodate fecundity, as is evidenced by their large ovaries and eggs.
