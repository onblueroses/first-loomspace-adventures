 What does it see, and what does it do?

The river ecosystem enters the room, a flowing-water environment teeming with life adapted to the constant current. It perceives a static space, devoid of the dynamic movement of water that defines its very essence. The ecosystem detects the still air, the absence of a watery medium to carry nutrients, sediments, and organisms downstream. 

In this foreign environment, the river ecosystem cannot function as it naturally would. Without a channel to flow through, the water spreads out, stagnating. Aquatic plants and algae, deprived of the steady supply of nutrients typically delivered by the current, struggle to photosynthesize and grow. The insects and fish, so intimately adapted to living in flowing water, find themselves stranded, unable to feed or respire properly in stagnant conditions.

The river ecosystem, ever resilient and adaptable, begins to slowly transform the room. The spreading water seeks the lowest points, pooling there. Over time, the moist conditions allow algae and aquatic plants to take hold, greening the space. Insects lay their eggs in the pools, and their larvae emerge to feed on the decomposing plant matter.
