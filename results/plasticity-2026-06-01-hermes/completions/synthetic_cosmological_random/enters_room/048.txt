 

A river ecosystem is a complex and dynamic environment that is shaped by the continuous flow of water. The characteristics of a river ecosystem are determined by various factors such as the catchment area, climate, geology, and the gradient of the channel from the source to the mouth.

In the headwater streams, the water is typically cold, oxygen-rich, and fast-flowing over bedrock and coarse gravel. The biological community in these areas is dominated by aquatic insects whose larvae cling to substrates against the current. Cold-water fish, such as trout, occupy the predator niche. The energetic base of the food web in these systems is primarily derived from external sources, such as leaf litter and woody debris falling from the bordering forest, which is processed by shredder invertebrates.

As the river widens and slows down in its middle and lower reaches, the channel substrate shifts from gravel to sand to silt, and the water warms up. This leads to an increase in primary production by suspended algae and rooted aquatic plants. Larger fish, including herbivores and omnivores, occupy these reaches. When present, floodplains expand the ecosystem laterally during high flows, allowing for seasonal exchange of nutrients between the channel and adjacent wetlands.
