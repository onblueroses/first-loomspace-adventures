 The river ecosystem is characterized by the unidirectional flow of water from upstream to downstream. This flow shapes the physical, chemical, and biological characteristics of the river. The source of the river, typically in highland areas, is called the headwaters. Here, the water is usually cold, clear, and fast-flowing due to the steep gradient. The substrate is often composed of bedrock, boulders, and coarse gravel.

As the river flows downstream, it transitions through several zones, each with distinct features. The upper reaches are characterized by fast-flowing, well-oxygenated water and a substrate dominated by gravel and cobbles. Aquatic insects, particularly those adapted to cling to the substrate in fast currents, dominate the invertebrate community. Fish species in this zone are typically cold-water species such as trout that require high oxygen levels.

In the middle reaches, the river widens, and the gradient decreases, resulting in slower flow. The substrate becomes finer, with sand and silt replacing gravel. The water temperature increases as the river is exposed to more sunlight. Primary production by algae and aquatic plants increases, providing a food source for a diverse community of invertebrates and fish.
