 

The river ecosystem is a dynamic and complex network of living organisms and their interactions with the physical environment in a flowing water system. This ecosystem is characterized by the unidirectional flow of water, which shapes the biological community and influences the distribution of species and nutrients.

Rivers and streams are classified as lotic ecosystems, which are distinguished from lentic ecosystems such as lakes and ponds by the continuous downstream transport of water, dissolved minerals, particulate organic matter, and living organisms. The nature of a river ecosystem is determined by several factors, including the catchment area, the climate of the region, the underlying geology, and the gradient of the channel from the source to the mouth.

In the headwater streams, the water is typically cold, oxygen-rich, and fast-flowing over bedrock and coarse gravel. The biological community in these areas is dominated by aquatic insects, whose larvae cling to substrates against the current. Cold-water fish, such as trout, occupy the predator niche in these ecosystems. The primary source of organic matter in headwater streams is from outside the system, in the form of leaf litter and woody debris falling from the bordering forest. This organic matter forms the energetic base of the food web through shredder invertebrates.
