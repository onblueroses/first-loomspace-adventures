 

The biological community of a river ecosystem is influenced by the physical environment of the channel, which is shaped by the watershed and the climate. In turn, the biological community alters the physical environment through feedback loops.

Headwater streams, which receive water from rain, snowmelt, and groundwater seepage, are generally cold, fast-flowing, and well-oxygenated. The channel is often shaded by trees, which limit primary production within the stream. The primary source of organic matter is leaf litter and woody debris that falls into the stream from the surrounding forest. This material is broken down by shredder invertebrates, which are in turn consumed by larger invertebrates and fish. The substrate in these streams is typically bedrock or coarse gravel, which provides habitat for aquatic insects such as mayflies, stoneflies, and caddisflies.

As the river widens and slows in its middle and lower reaches, the channel substrate becomes finer, shifting from gravel to sand to silt. The water warms and becomes more turbid, with suspended particles limiting light penetration. Primary production increases, with suspended algae and rooted aquatic plants contributing to the food web. Larger fish, including herbivores and omnivores, occupy these reaches, feeding on aquatic plants
