 

The river continuum concept (RCC) provides a unifying framework for understanding the structure and function of river ecosystems. The RCC proposes that changes in physical conditions and resource availability along the river channel, from headwaters to mouth, selectively control the biological community and associated ecosystem processes. The model is based on the longitudinal gradient of the river system, from small headwater streams to large lowland rivers, and emphasizes the gradual shift from allochthony (dependence on external organic matter) to autochthony (dependence on internal primary production) along the river continuum.

In the headwaters, the model predicts that allochthonous inputs of coarse particulate organic matter (CPOM), such as leaves and wood, dominate energy inputs. These materials are broken down by shredding invertebrates, such as aquatic insects, and decomposer microorganisms, such as fungi and bacteria. The products of this breakdown, fine particulate organic matter (FPOM) and dissolved organic matter (DOM), are transported downstream. In the mid-reaches, with increased width and light penetration, autochthonous production by algae and aquatic plants becomes more important, supporting a diverse community of collectors, grazers, and predators.
