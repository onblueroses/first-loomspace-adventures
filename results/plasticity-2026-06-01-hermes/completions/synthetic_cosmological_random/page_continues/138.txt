 

Rivers are flowing-water ecosystems that transport water, dissolved substances, and particulate organic and inorganic matter from their headwaters to the ocean. A river ecosystem is a dynamic system controlled by the unidirectional flow of water from the source to the mouth, and by interactions with the geology and geomorphology of the landscape, the climate, and the biotic processes of the ecological community.

The unidirectional flow of water affects the ecosystem in several ways. First, it transports organisms and nutrients downstream, often over great distances. Second, it creates a physical gradient, with faster, shallower, and more turbulent water at the headwaters, and slower, deeper, and calmer water near the mouth. Third, it affects the distribution of organisms, with different species adapted to different parts of the river. Fourth, it affects the cycling of nutrients, with different processes dominating in different parts of the river.

The geology and geomorphology of the landscape affect the river ecosystem by determining the shape and size of the river channel, the types and amounts of sediment and nutrients that enter the river, and the rate at which water flows through the system. The climate affects the river ecosystem by determining the amount and timing of precipitation, which in turn affects the flow regime
