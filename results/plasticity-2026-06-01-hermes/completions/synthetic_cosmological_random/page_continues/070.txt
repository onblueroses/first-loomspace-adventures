 

The unidirectional flow of water defines river ecosystems as fundamentally different from other water bodies. The physical structure of a river channel, the types of organisms found there, and the cycling of nutrients through the system are all influenced by the movement of water downstream. The flow of water shapes the channel, scouring out pools and riffles, eroding and depositing substrates, and creating a dynamic mosaic of habitats. The velocity of flow, the frequency of floods, and the degree of connectivity between the channel and floodplain all govern the types of organisms that inhabit the river and the strategies they use to survive.

River ecosystems are typically divided into three zones based on the physical characteristics of the channel and the dominant biological communities. The headwaters, or upper reaches, are often steep and narrow, with cold, fast-flowing water tumbling over rocks and waterfalls. This zone is dominated by aquatic insects and other invertebrates that cling to the substrate and filter organic matter from the water. The middle reaches are wider and slower, with deeper pools and a more diverse substrate. Here, the community shifts to include more fish species and a greater variety of invertebrates.
