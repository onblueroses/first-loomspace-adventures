 

At the source of a river system, headwater streams are typically narrow, steep, and fast-flowing, with cold, oxygen-rich water. The channel substrate is often bedrock or coarse gravel, with abundant boulders and cobbles. The flow regime is characterized by high variability, with sudden surges in discharge following heavy rainfall or snowmelt events. The biological community in headwater streams is dominated by aquatic insects, particularly the larvae of caddisflies, stoneflies, and mayflies, which cling to the substrate and feed on fine organic particles or prey on other invertebrates. Cold-water fish species such as trout are the top predators. Because of the limited primary production in shaded headwater streams, the food web relies largely on inputs of leaf litter and woody debris from the surrounding forest. Shredder invertebrates break down this coarse organic matter, making it available to other consumers.

In the middle reaches of a river, the channel typically widens and deepens, and the gradient decreases, resulting in slower water velocities. The substrate shifts from gravel to sand and silt, and the water warms as it is exposed to more sunlight.
