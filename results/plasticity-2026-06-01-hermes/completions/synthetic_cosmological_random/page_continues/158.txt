 

Rivers are flowing bodies of water that transport water, dissolved minerals, particulate organic matter, and living organisms from the headwaters to the mouth. The unidirectional movement of water shapes the biological community and ecosystem processes along the way. Rivers and streams are considered lotic ecosystems, in contrast to lentic ecosystems like lakes and ponds where water is relatively stationary.

The character of a river ecosystem is determined by several factors, including the catchment area (the land area that drains into the river), regional climate, underlying geology, and the gradient or slope of the river channel from its source to its mouth. 

In headwater streams, the water is typically cold, oxygen-rich, and flows rapidly over bedrock and coarse gravel. The biological community here is dominated by aquatic insects whose larvae cling to the substrate to avoid being swept away by the current. Cold-water fish like trout occupy the predator niche. Most of the organic matter in these systems comes from outside the stream, in the form of leaf litter and woody debris falling from the bordering forest. This external organic matter forms the energetic base of the food web, supporting shredder invertebrates that break down the leaf litter.
