 

The general flow pattern of rivers—where water moves from high elevations to lower ones—creates a physical template for ecological processes. Although headwater streams comprise a small fraction of the river network length, they drain a majority of the catchment area. Thus, the transport of water and materials is largely downhill, from small streams to larger ones. This unidirectional flow of water creates a strong longitudinal linkage within the ecosystem, known as the river continuum concept. Downstream communities are shaped by upstream processes, and any modifications to the channel or the riparian zone can affect all downstream habitats.

The unidirectional flow of water also results in an efficient transfer of energy and nutrients from upstream to downstream. In headwater streams, most energy is derived from allochthonous sources, meaning it originates from outside the channel. This external organic matter, such as leaf litter and woody debris, is broken down by aquatic decomposers, including bacteria and fungi, which are in turn consumed by invertebrates. These invertebrates are then eaten by larger predators, including fish.
