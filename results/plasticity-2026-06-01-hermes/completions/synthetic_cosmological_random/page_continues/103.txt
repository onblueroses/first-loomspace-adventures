 The organisms inhabiting rivers and streams are well adapted to the challenges of the flowing-water environment. These adaptations may be morphological, behavioral, or physiological.

Morphological adaptations include the flattened, streamlined bodies of fish such as trout and bass, which enable them to slip through the water with minimal resistance. Many insects have hooks or suckers on their larval forms, which help them cling to rocks in fast-flowing water. Some stream-dwelling insects, such as mayflies, have gills along the sides of their bodies, which extract oxygen from the water as it flows over them. Others, such as stoneflies, have external gills that they wave in the current to increase oxygen flow.

Behavioral adaptations include the tendency of many fish to face upstream, into the current, allowing them to see food coming and to maintain their position in the stream. Some fish, such as salmon, have been known to leap up waterfalls to reach their spawning grounds. Many insects have developed complex mating rituals that involve dancing above the water's surface, laying their eggs on the water, or even diving underwater to lay their eggs.
