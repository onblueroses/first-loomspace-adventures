 

## Biological communities

The biological communities of rivers and streams are structured by the unidirectional flow of water, which transports sediments, organic matter, and organisms downstream. The environmental conditions in a river change from its source to its mouth, resulting in different assemblages of organisms along its length. These changes occur over long distances and time, and are known as longitudinal gradients. 

The living components of a river ecosystem are the biotic factors, and the nonliving parts are the abiotic factors. The biotic factors of a river ecosystem include the various organisms that live in or around the river, such as fish, insects, birds, and plants. The abiotic factors include the physical and chemical conditions of the river, such as water temperature, flow, pH, and dissolved oxygen levels.

Rivers are home to a diverse array of organisms, including fish, invertebrates, and plants. The specific species found in a river depend on factors such as water temperature, flow, and the availability of food and habitat. Some common species found in rivers include trout, salmon, bass, and catfish, as well as insects such as mayflies, caddisflies, and stoneflies.
