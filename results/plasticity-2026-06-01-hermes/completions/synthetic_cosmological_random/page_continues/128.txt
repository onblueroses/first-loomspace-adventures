 “The biological community is dominated by aquatic insects whose larvae cling to substrates against the current; cold-water fish such as trout occupy the predator niche. Organic matter enters these systems largely from outside, in the form of leaf litter and woody debris falling from the bordering forest, which forms the energetic base of the food web through shredder invertebrates.”

The streams in my region of the Northeast US are headwater streams. I have seen this ecosystem change in my lifetime, and in many ways it is a cautionary tale. I grew up fishing these waters for brook trout, the only trout native to the Eastern US. As the streams have warmed due to climate change, and as farming practices have changed, the brook trout have largely been replaced by brown trout, a European import. Brown trout tolerate warmer water than brook trout, and they also require less oxygen. The warming water and the lower oxygen levels are a direct result of agricultural runoff and the loss of streamside vegetation. The loss of the brook trout is just one indication of the loss of stream health. There are many other indicators, including the decline of mayflies, stoneflies, and caddisflies, all of which are aquatic insects whose larvae cling to substrates against the current.
