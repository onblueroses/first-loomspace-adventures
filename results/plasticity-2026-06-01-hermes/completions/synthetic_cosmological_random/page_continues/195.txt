 

The ecology of rivers is shaped by the unidirectional flow of water and the transport of dissolved and particulate substances from upstream to downstream. The physical environment of a river ecosystem is determined by the channel substrate, water velocity, temperature, and chemistry. These factors influence the distribution of organisms and functional processes within the river ecosystem.

Headwater streams are often characterized by cold, clear water, fast flow, and a substrate of bedrock and coarse gravel. The primary energy source in these streams is typically allochthonous organic matter, such as leaf litter and woody debris, derived from the riparian vegetation. This organic matter is broken down by shredder invertebrates, such as stonefly and caddisfly larvae, which serve as the base of the food web. Cold-water fish species, such as trout, are often the top predators in these systems.

As the river widens and slows in its middle and lower reaches, the substrate shifts to sand and silt, and the water warms. Primary production by suspended algae and rooted aquatic plants increases, providing an autochthonous energy source. Larger fish species, including herbivores and omnivores, are more common in these reaches. Floodplains, when present, expand the ecosystem laterally
