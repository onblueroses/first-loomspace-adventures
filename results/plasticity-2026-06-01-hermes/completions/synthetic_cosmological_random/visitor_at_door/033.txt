 'I am a stranger here,' he says. 'I would like to spend the night.' It is a clear, cold night, and the snow lies deep upon the ground. You bid him welcome, and give him of your best. As you cease to be a stranger, he may become a friend. The stranger within your gates is the stream that flows at the bottom of your land. His name is the River. He walks in the valley, and his voice fills the valley. In the stillness of the night, in the quiet of the day, you hear him, and you become acquainted. He is a friendly stream, and soon you call him by name. He becomes to you Otter River, or Pine, or Hemlock Brook. You love him, and you are glad he is no stranger. He is a friend, and you treat him as a friend. You are careful to safeguard him from harm. You soon find he is a part of your life, and you cannot do without him. You find that he has become a part of you, and you have become a part of him.

—Herbert O. Hopkins, The Lay of the Land, 1914
