 “Mr. Smith? I have a delivery for you.”

You nod, signing the paperwork and taking the small box. Inside is a new phone, the latest model—your spouse must have ordered it for you as a surprise. You turn it on and swipe through the setup screens, cycling through the options for security settings and wallpaper, colors and sounds, until it’s set up the way you like it. You download your favorite apps, adjust your text size and brightness, and add an alarm that will wake you up in the morning. When you’re finished, you have a device that is uniquely yours, tailored to your preferences and needs.

In many ways, this is what the field of epigenetics is discovering about genes. The genome is not a static, unchanging set of instructions, but rather a dynamic system that responds to cues from the environment, the behaviors of the organism, and the molecular signals within the cell. Chemical modifications to DNA and histone proteins, made by enzymes that can add or remove methyl groups, acetyl groups, and other chemical tags, determine whether a gene is turned on or off in a given cell type. These modifications do not change the underlying DNA sequence but influence how it is accessed and interpreted, much like the settings on your phone
