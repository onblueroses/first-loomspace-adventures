 "Hello," he says, "I'm here to talk about the river."

The river has been a source of life, of sustenance, of transportation and communication, of cleansing and renewal, for the community for generations. The people have fished in its waters, irrigated their crops from its flow, floated their goods to market on its current, and refreshed themselves bodily and spiritually in its cool, clear depths.

But now, the visitor explains, the river is sick. The fish are fewer and smaller. The water runs cloudy and smells foul. Algae grow in choking mats, blocking the sunlight. The once-thriving ecosystem is out of balance, and the people are suffering.

The visitor is a scientist, a student of river ecosystems, and he has come to help the community understand what is happening to their river and what they can do to restore it to health. He explains the delicate interplay of physical, chemical, and biological factors that sustain the river's vitality - the flow regime that shapes the channel, the nutrient balance that feeds the food web, the riparian vegetation that stabilizes the banks and shades the water, the diverse community of organisms from bacteria to fish that process nutrients and energy and maintain the system's resilience.
