 He is a tall man, with a beard, and his eyes are red and swollen. He has a nervous, fidgety manner.

"I have come," he says, "to ask you to sing at my wife's funeral. It is to be to-morrow. I will pay you well."

"I am sorry," answers the singer, "but I cannot sing at funerals. It would be too much for me. I would die."

The man at the door speaks again, brokenly, pleadingly.

"I have heard you sing," he says, "and it was like hearing an angel from heaven. I never heard such a voice before. I believe that the dead can hear, and I want my wife to hear you. She was fond of music. You will not refuse. I would give you anything."

The singer replies, hesitatingly, "I am very poor. I have suffered much. I have not been able to sing for a long time. I am not sure that I could sing now."

The man at the door catches at this. "But try," he pleads. "Try to sing to-morrow. I will come for you in a carriage. You shall have everything you need--rest, and warmth, and food.
