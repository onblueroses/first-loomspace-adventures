 It is John Greenleaf Whittier, the Quaker poet. He has come to ask Mrs. Stowe to write a story about slavery, a story that will make the whole nation feel what an accursed thing it is. "You must be the one to tell the story, because you have been on the very border of slavery and seen it in all its worst features. You believe that 'a hair of the head of John Brown would outweigh in the balance the whole body of all the rest of them,'—the leaders of the system of slavery. You believe that the best way to help the cause of freedom is to 'make this whole nation feel what an accursed thing slavery is.' You must write the story! You can help the cause, as no one else can!"

She promised to think about it. It was a startling proposition. She pondered it day and night. She had been thinking deeply on the subject of slavery for many years. She had talked with the abolitionists of the East and of the West. She had heard the stories of the fugitive slaves; she had listened to the arguments of the pro-slavery people; she had read the political speeches on both sides.
